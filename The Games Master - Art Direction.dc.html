§ 01 · The reframe

He is not trying to win.

He is trying to lose — and he can't.

Aldric Voss is bound to this house and bound to win. He cheats because he is compelled to — the curse won't let him throw a game. But a win keeps him here. Only a loss sets him free, and he cannot lose on purpose.

So he does the only thing left to a man who can't open the cage from the inside: he leaves the key where you might trip over it. Every cheat is a tell he can't help. Every impossible event is him showing his hand on purpose while pretending it's an accident. He wants to be caught. He needs you to be better than him.

This single idea rewires the art direction. Horror games make the antagonist more monstrous as they escalate. Ours does the opposite: the further things break, the more of the man leaks through the cracks. Corruption isn't cruelty ramping up. It's a mask failing.

Three tenets that fall out of it
Gold is a hand reaching out.

The palette calls gold "the trustworthy host." Keep that — but weaponize it. Any gold in the world (not the UI) is a breadcrumb Aldric planted. Players learn to hunt it.

Green is relief, not victory.

When you catch him, the "caught" green (#40F259) is his emotion, not yours. It should read almost holy — the one honest color in the house.

The whole game is one question.

Who is the next Games Master? Replacement, Cheat, the faceless first-person Labyrinth, the Mirror — they all point one way. Direct every room toward that dread.

THE FIX — two reds, two jobs

Bruised crimson (#8F2F2A) — the wound colour. Impossible events, tarnish, a mismatched frame, felt bleeding at high corruption. Sickly, not loud. This is him.

Pure alarm (#F22626) — reserved. Only true danger: the Huntsman's proximity, the sanity-critical pulse, the Hollow's last game. When the player sees this red, it always means the house wants to hurt you — never just "a cheat happened."

MATERIAL — the mixed-fidelity stack is a feature

Kenney furniture + Quaternius low-poly characters against the bespoke PBR Study pack isn't a compromise to hide. Lean into it: the set is real, the people are not. Detailed rooms, hand-textured props, PBR wood and brass — but every human figure (jury, Huntsman, portraits' subjects) stays stylized and low-poly.

That gap does dramatic work: the world is more solid than the souls in it. Aldric is the one figure who earns higher fidelity — because he's the one who was once real.

§ 03 · Aldric Voss

The host, and the man under him.

You never see his face resolve until an ending demands it. What you read instead is posture, the light on him, and how the room behaves around him. Seven states, seven recipes — each buildable from the shaders already shipping (rim/outline, vignette, horror_chromatic, screen-shake) plus one practical light.

SILHOUETTE RULES
  • Tall, seated more than standing — a man who hosts, doesn't chase.
  • Formal Victorian, immaculate — but one detail always wrong (a glove that never comes off, a watch stopped, cufflinks that are card pips).
  • Hands are the tell surface. We light them separately.
  • Face stays in shadow / rim only. When it clears, it's an ending.

§ 05 · The three legacy rooms

Court. Gallery. Labyrinth.

Still on the old 2D base — the biggest open canvas. Each gets one committed direction, each ties to the others, each must hold up beside the Study. And they must feel like their own games, not reskins.

The Court

"The Assize of One"

A real courtroom — bench, dock, jury gallery — but every station is him. Prosecutor, judge, bailiff, jury foreman. The staging swings a hard practical light to whichever role is speaking; the rest drops to gaslamp gloom. You are the one figure who never moves, in the one seat always lit.

THE TIE-IN
The jury is the Gallery portraits — past guests, watching you stand where they stood.
THE CLUE
Evidence Cards are the breadcrumbs he planted. Present the true one and his gavel's gold tarnishes — the tell that he's rigged this for you.
⚠ PUSHBACK

Don't build a full courtroom — it fights manor cohesion and the asset budget, and it breaks the Clue-style "one house" premise. Make it the dining hall re-dressed as a tribunal: candelabra become the bench, chairs become the jury box, the long table is the bar. It reads as him staging a play — which is exactly what a courtroom-for-one is. Reuses Kenney set dressing instead of new hero geometry.

HUD · three arguments = three wax seals that break as you win them. Evidence fans at the frame's lower edge.

The Gallery

"The Collection"

A long portrait hall where you match pairs — and the GM doesn't play. He watches. The horror is his absence: his gold dialogue label appears with no one there to speak it. Each portrait has its own picture-light that gutters and dies when you look away, so the room darkens as your memory is tested. You are matching the dead.

THE SECRET
One portrait is Aldric as a guest — young, unmasked, the original winner who became the host. It's the answer to the whole game, hidden in plain sight.
THE SECRET DOOR
Match his portrait and it swings open — the hidden room the Cheat true ending requires. This room is the true-ending gate.
✓ WHY THIS ROOM WINS

It's the cheapest room to build well. Portraits are 2D textures on 3D frames — it sidesteps the character-model fidelity problem entirely. This is where gold lives thickest (his trophy room, the mask at its proudest), and where you should spend the polish you save elsewhere.

The Labyrinth

"The House Between"

First person, always. You never see your body, your hands, your gender — you could be anyone. You could be the player. The 7×7 maze is the manor's hidden underside: servant passages, dust, geometry a few degrees wrong. The Huntsman stalks it, never fully seen — a silhouette that could be you a beat behind. 30 turns before he catches you.

THE IDENTITY HOOK
The only mirrors in the game are here. They show nothing — or the GM — or a figure that mimics you a beat late. This is where the game's thesis lives.
ASSET = MECHANIC
A sliding-tile maze is a modular Kenney corridor kit rearranged. The mechanic and the build strategy are the same object.
⚠ DELIBERATE PALETTE VIOLATION

The warm gold base cannot carry cold spatial dread — it'll fight you. So break it, on purpose: drain the Labyrinth to bone-and-ash, push the manor brown cool and grey, kill the gaslamp warmth. Reintroduce gold only as the exit beacon and clue glints. The break is the point — you've stepped behind the part of the house he keeps warm for guests. Flagging it loudly so it doesn't read as a palette bug.

Low corruption: crisp brass, all four pips gold. High: the hairline tarnishes to bruised crimson, corners crack, one pip flickers green. You can't turn the frame off — so it's a corruption gauge you always see.

THE TELL AFFORDANCE — let the player watch him back

The BehaviorAnalyzer reads the player. Reciprocate: when a tell is available, the screen holds its breath — a faint vignette closes and a single gold glint catches at frame edge. Hold [Read] to call it. Land it and the caught-green blooms — his relief, your progress. This is the mechanical heart of "he wants to be caught," and it gives the player a reason to notice when they're being noticed.

The verb is earned, not given. Early on the affordance does not exist — he's too good, his tells sub-perceptual, and the player simply loses hands they know they'd won. That builds a Suspicion the player can feel but can't act on. Only after they run their own test — baiting a hand where an honest opponent couldn't win, and watching him cheat to take it — does Read unlock for the rest of the game. The playable Parlor prototype in this project models exactly this: blind phase → suspicion → test → the verb.

§ 08 · The Manor layer

Between the games is where he talks to you.

The exploration layer isn't filler between games — it's the channel Aldric uses when he can't say anything directly. Puzzles you solve with your hands, not the game modes. Clues he plants. Doors that open because you did something, not because you found a key on the floor. This is the replay engine and the true-ending path.

Gold = his voice

The one rule that teaches itself. Any gold in the world — not the UI — is a breadcrumb he left. A gilded book spine, a glint under a rug. Players learn within an hour to hunt gold, and every hunt is him reaching out.

Puzzles use the games' vocabulary

Tune the Study's viola to a phrase from his VO → a panel opens. Set the dice to a date. Arrange the altered chess pieces as a lock. Anagram a Court evidence word. The manor puzzles rhyme with the games without being the games.

Doors open because you acted

Match his hidden Gallery portrait; checkmate with the Arbiter specifically; balance the Defiance/Compliance scale in the hall. Secret passages are earned by doing, and the hidden room they lead to is the Cheat ending's requirement.

Key fragments = his contract

Reframe the floor-gating key fragments as torn pieces of Aldric's original contract — the deal that bound him. Assembling them isn't just progression; it's you reading the fine print he can't recite aloud.

HOUSE STYLE FOR SCARES — earn the flinch, don't buy it

No loud stings. Every surprise is a "something is different and I'm not sure I'm right" — a chair moved when you return, a portrait's new expression, the fake Huntsman at the end of a hall that's gone when you look again, your own footsteps arriving a beat late. This is gaslamp dread, and it doubles as sanity pressure: the scare and the doubt are the same event. Save the one or two genuine jumps for moments the reframe earns them — the mirror where Aldric mouths "let me lose."

§ 10 · Where I'd push it

Eight games, or eight reskins.

You asked what else. Here's where I'd take the whole thing — the gap between "card games in a spooky house" and something a player finishes and immediately restarts. Two rules under all of it: every game earns its own verb, and every system serves the one secret — he wants to lose.

The signature each game needs
Parlor
CARDS · HOME BASE

The four suits should do things. Eyes peek his hand, Teeth force a discard, Bones replay from the pile, Flames burn a trick. This is also the tutorial for reading tells — the room where players learn the game's real skill.

Bones
DICE · SEEDY

Let the player inspect a die between rolls — rotate it, find the filed edge or the heavy pip. Push-your-luck with a physical accusation built in. Catching a loaded die is a caught-green beat you earned with your hands.

Study
CHESS · THE BAR

The Arbiter piece can overrule one of your moves — his single legal cheat, on the board where you can see it happen. He narrates the game as a metaphor for your run, and it's never quite wrong.

Gallery
MEMORY · WATCHED

Not just pairs — match each guest to how they ended (a fate-plaque under the frame). Lore becomes the mechanic. Linger on one too long and the watched-meter fills; he speaks from nowhere.

Court
WORD TRIAL · ACCUSED

Your evidence is what you found exploring — the manor arms you for trial. Cross-examine, press his statements, catch a contradiction. Every contradiction you land is a tell he can't take back.

Labyrinth
MAZE · SPATIAL

The sliding maze is the manor's floor plan. Reshaping it here reshapes rooms you'll walk later. Identity horror + spatial dread + a quiet meta hook, all in one mechanic.

Wager
DEAL · FAUSTIAN

The UI itself lies. The stated cost is shown; the hidden catch is legible only if you've been hunting gold — a gold clause in the fine print. Attentive players read the trap; the rest sign it.

Mirror
NG+ · YOUR GHOST

You play your recorded first run back at yourself. The horror: your ghost makes your old mistakes — or plays better than you remember being. The one opponent who cheats exactly like you do.

The house remembers — systemic & meta
The save file is a character.

It persists across deletes. He references your last run. The Mirror and the NG+ fifth corruption tier are built from it. Inscryption / DDLC lineage — but here it's earned by the reframe: a trapped man would remember everyone who failed to free him.

He learns your name.

Reads your profile name and uses it — wrong at first (the Tell system warming up), then exactly right once he's "seen" you. The Cheat ending: your own name, in his voice.

The stopped clock.

One grandfather clock, frozen at the minute Aldric won and got trapped. Escape restarts it. Every other ending lets it finally chime. A single prop that carries the whole tragedy.

The voice under the voice.

His host-VO has a true voice beneath it. At VULNERABLE and high corruption, a half-second of the real man bleeds through — the audio tell matching the green-through-gold visual one. And pausing never truly pauses: he waits, watching, and comments if you linger.

Things worth finding — the side layer
The other guests

Luggage, a child's toy, a wedding ring. Every Gallery portrait had a room in this house. Optional, findable, and the emotional ballast the horror needs to land.

A friend in the walls

A second presence that isn't him, leaving the real clues. Aldric's earlier self? A previous near-escapee? Keep it ambiguous — a reason to feel less alone, and to doubt that feeling.

The cheat catalog

A notebook that logs every cheat you catch — a compendium you fill. Completing it is the true ending's 8-cheats requirement, so the mystery has a visible collection goal driving replay.

Cross-game ciphers

A lock that needs a number from Bones, a word from Court, a suit from Parlor. One puzzle that forces you to touch every mode — the manor stitching its games into a single mystery.

Why you replay — the intrigue engine
The tagline is the first clue.

You were right to pull "he cheats." "He's never lost" is a fact the player can observe — it hands them nothing. Discovering how he's never lost, then why he keeps winning when he'd give anything to stop, is the entire game. Never state the cheat. Let them catch it.

Seven is a lie too.

"Seven games." There are eight — the Mirror. The number itself is the opening lie, sitting in the title the whole time. Noticing it is the mystery's first move, and it costs you nothing to plant.

Six keyholes, one hidden room.

Let players see all six endings exist before they know how to reach them — six locks in a room found early. And frame difficulty as his mood, not a menu: you choose how much of him greets you, and the fiction never breaks.

§ 11 · The story underneath

Whoever beats the Games Master becomes him.

Everything so far needs one spine to hang on, or it's atmosphere without a reason. Here it is. The manor is a cycle. Aldric was a guest once — he won, and the house made him the host. That's the curse: the prize is the cage. He cheats because the house makes the host cheat, and he can't stop, and he can't lose, and the only door out is a player who understands what winning here actually costs.

The three layers the player peels
I
THE SUSPICION
He cheats.

The first thing you catch. The tells, the impossible deals, the pieces that move. Most players get here on their own within the first two games. This layer is just "the house is not fair."

II
THE TURN
He wants to be caught.

The clues are too convenient. The green flickers. The losing hand dealt face-up. The realization flips the whole game: he isn't your enemy, he's a prisoner leaving you the key, and he can't say so.

III
THE COST
If you win, you're next.

The deepest layer, and the one the endings live on. Beating him the ordinary way just makes you the new Master. Freeing him — and yourself — means understanding the trap well enough to refuse the chair.

The friend in the walls — who it is

Commit to an answer, keep it ambiguous to the player: the friend is the host before Aldric — the one he beat and replaced. The house never fully let that one go. It's a residue, a memory worn into the walls, and it wants the cycle to end so it can finally rest too.

Mechanically, this is where the save file earns its keep: the friend's clue-drops are seeded from your previous runs. On a first run it's faint and cryptic. On repeat runs it "remembers" — it points you faster, because it has watched you fail before. The friend and the Mirror are the same technology pointed in opposite directions: one helps, one haunts.

Aldric can't help you directly — the curse forbids it. The friend can, because the house has already forgotten it's there.

THE CYCLE
A guest arrives
The guest wins
The house makes them the host
They wait for the next guest…
Or someone understands, and refuses the chair.
§ 12 · Sound & silence

The house is quiet, so you hear yourself.

Gaslamp dread is carried more by ears than eyes. The rule matches the visuals: no stings, no orchestral jump cues. Music is scarce and almost entirely diegetic — it comes from objects in the house, so when it stops, a real thing went quiet.

The voice under the voice

Aldric's host-VO is warm, measured, performed. Beneath it, on the same line, a second dry track — the real man. At VULNERABLE and high corruption you hear a half-second of him underneath the mask. The audio twin of green-through-gold.

The score is furniture

The Study viola. A music box in a nursery you find. The grandfather clock. That's the soundtrack — real sources, real positions in 3D space. No non-diegetic score until an ending breaks the rule on purpose.

Sanity remixes the mix

Below 50, your own heartbeat enters the low end. Below 25, your moves echo a beat late — a card laid twice, footsteps that arrive after you stop. The horror is hearing yourself do things you don't remember doing.

Corruption detunes

As the tier climbs, the diegetic sources drift flat — the viola a quarter-tone sour, the clock's tick uneven. The house's music slips out of tune the way his composure does. By tier 4, it's almost silence and room tone.

Silence is the loudest tool

The scares are absences: the clock stops mid-tick, the room tone cuts to a vacuum for two seconds, then returns. Save the one true audio jump for the mirror where the real voice, unmasked, says "let me lose."

He hears you too

Long pauses draw a comment. Rushing draws a slower, colder line. If the game can read a mic, even a real gasp could earn a glance — but keep that optional; the fiction shouldn't depend on hardware.

§ 14 · Build order for one person

The honest sequence, solo.

None of this matters if it doesn't get built. You're one person mid-migration to 3D, and the brief already names Court, Gallery and Labyrinth as the legacy three. Here's the order I'd actually work in — cheapest wins first, biggest risk last, each step producing something playable.

⚠ THE REAL RISK

The scope trap for this game isn't art — it's the eight distinct game modes. Every one is its own ruleset, AI, and UI. Protect the schedule by making the manor/exploration layer and one excellent game carry the demo, not all eight half-built.

1
Lock the vertical slice: Study + the corruption language on it.

You already have the Study as the bar. Build the tier 0→3 drift on the room you've finished before touching new rooms. This proves the whole visual thesis in the one space that's ready.

2
Gallery next — it's the cheapest room that looks expensive.

Portraits are 2D on 3D frames; no opponent AI to build (the GM only watches). It sidesteps the character-fidelity problem entirely and delivers a full horror room for a fraction of the others' cost. Bank an early, striking win.

3
Court as a re-dressed dining hall, not new geometry.

Reuse an existing manor room and swing lights to fake the tribunal. The word-trial logic is the real work; don't spend it on a bespoke courtroom set you'll build once and light poorly.

4
Labyrinth last — it's the biggest, and it can wait.

Real-time stalking AI, spatial 3D, the deliberate palette break — it's the highest-risk room and the least like the others. Prototype it grey-box with the modular corridor kit long before you dress it. If the schedule slips, it's the one that can ship in a later update.

5
Thread the manor layer through all of it, continuously.

Gold clues, the cheat catalog, the friend in the walls, the stopped clock — build these alongside each room, not as a final pass. They're what turns four rooms into one haunted house, and they're mostly script and placement, not new tech.

§ 16 · The first ten minutes

Teach the lie without a tutorial.

No pop-ups, no "press X to accuse." The opening is the teach: the game shows you fairness so you'll feel its absence, then hands you the verb by letting you succeed once. Beat sheet.

0:00
The deal.

First Parlor hand, tier 0, scrupulously fair. He's CHARMING; the rules teach themselves through play. No cheat yet — you're establishing "honest" as the baseline the whole game will violate.

~2:00
The first slip.

One tiny, deniable cheat — a card that shouldn't have won. No Read prompt. Just a wrong-feeling beat and a half-second of the tell. Let it pass. Plant the doubt and trust the player to carry it.

~4:00
The test — and the verb.

You still can't accuse him — he's too good, and you know it. But the doubt has a shape now, so you set a trap: bait a hand where an honest player couldn't win, and watch what he does. The cheat surfaces in the open, the Read verb unlocks for good, and the first caught-green lands with his IRRITATED crack. The player earns the ability to accuse by proving it themselves.

~6:00
The gold.

First step into the manor between games. A gold glint catches on a book spine; following it finds the first clue. You've now taught "gold in the world = his voice" without a single line of instruction.

~8:00
The cost.

A lore fragment about a previous guest — a name, a portrait, a room that was theirs. Layer III planted quietly: people came before you, and something happened to them here.

~10:00
The choice.

A first fork — a small Wager, or a moment to push back or play along. The Defiance/Compliance balance visibly moves for the first time. End the intro on agency: you understand the game is rigged, you have a verb to fight it, and your choices are being counted.

§ 17 · The house remembers — the full system

You can close the game. You can't leave the house.

This is the spine that makes the whole thing an Inscryption-class object and not just a card game with a story. The manor persists — across runs, across deaths, across deleting your save. Aldric is bound to the house; the house keeps a ledger. Crucially, per the reframe: the memory helps as much as it haunts. A trapped man who wants out would want you to remember what nearly worked last time.

THE ONE PRINCIPLE

Persistence is not a difficulty setting. It is the narrative engine and the replay hook, and every use of it must feel like the house knowing you — never like a stats screen. If the player can't feel it as Aldric's memory, cut it.

1 · The Ledger — what the house keeps

One small local file, separate from your normal save slot. It survives "New Game" and it survives deleting your save — the way Inscryption keeps a second file the uninstall doesn't touch. It records not scores but habits.

How you play

Aggressive or cautious, fast or hesitant, which suits you favour, whether you bluff. This is what the Mirror is built from.

What you caught

Cheats caught vs. let stand, false accusations, which of his tells you've learned to read. He adapts to hide the ones you know.

How you left

Every ending reached, every death, and the near-misses — the door you reached and didn't open, the shard you held and lost.

Who you are

Your OS/profile name, your total time in the house, which clues you've already found so he never re-explains them.

2 · How it surfaces — memory made visible
He greets a returning guest

"You caught me at the dice last time. You won't, tonight." "You reached the door, and didn't open it. Why?" Specific, not generic. The single most powerful use of the ledger.

The Tell system starts warm

On run 2+ he already has data on you — adapts faster, hides the tells you've learned. But the friend in the walls also points faster. The house cuts both ways.

The Gallery grows

Got the Collection ending once? Your portrait is on the wall this run. The dead you're matching now include a previous you.

Rooms stay solved

A door you unlocked stays known; a puzzle you cracked sits already ajar. Replay is progress, not repetition — you resume the mystery, you don't re-grind it.

The clock counts you

The stopped grandfather clock reads a different time each run — quietly tallying your attempts. Players notice by run three, and it chills.

The Mirror is you

The NG+ opponent is the ledger's recording of your first run, played back. The friend and the Mirror are the same tech pointed opposite ways: one helps, one haunts.

3 · The deletion ladder — the Inscryption move

Escalate the fourth wall in three earned steps. Each one should be a genuine surprise, spaced runs apart — never a tutorial pop-up.

I
"New Game" stops meaning new.

After your first ending, starting over greets you as a returning guest, not a stranger. The main menu itself has changed a little. First crack in the fourth wall.

II
Deleting the save doesn't work.

The game lets you delete it. Next launch, Aldric is already waiting: "You tried to leave. The house doesn't work that way." The ledger persisted; the normal save didn't. This is the moment players screenshot.

III
The true ending is the only real reset.

Reaching the Cheat ending — freeing him — is the one act that genuinely wipes the ledger, because you broke the cycle. Only then does the game return to a clean slate, and only then do the credits finally roll. Freeing him frees the record.

4 · THE FOURTH-WALL RULES

He knows it's a game and that you can quit. He'll note a long pause, an alt-tab, your playtime ("You've been here four hours. Do they know where you are?"), and use your name. Rare, earned, never a gimmick — three or four times in a whole playthrough, each one landing because you'd stopped expecting it.

⚠ THE RESPONSIBLE VERSION

Persisting through deletion is powerful but can read as invasive, and it can trip cloud-save and anti-cheat oddness. Keep it one small, obvious local file — the game's declared gimmick, not a secret. And give a real in-world opt-out: "Burn the ledger" in options, for the player who genuinely wants the house to forget them. The trick only stays charming if it can be refused.

§ 18 · The Story — the treatment

Two men who both want to lose, and can't.

LOGLINE

A gambler with nothing left to lose is invited to a house where the host has never lost — and slowly realizes the only way either of them walks free is if he learns to beat a man who is begging, in secret, to be beaten.

THE MAN YOU PLAY · never named

You are never given his name. In the first-person games he has no face, no voice, no reflection — and by the end you'll understand that was the point. He could be anyone who owed too much and grieved too hard. He could be you. What we do know arrives in fragments, the way it does in the prologue walk: he is a good player, a bad husband's ghost of himself, and a father running out of road.

He owes forty-one thousand to men who don't take excuses, nine more to a bank, and a sum with too many zeroes to a surgeon in Zürich who can fix his daughter's heart. He is not here to gamble. He is here because it is the last door left, and the house knew that before he did.

HE CARRIES THREE GHOSTS INTO THE HOUSE
Mara — the wife

She kept the books. Kept him honest. Kept him. Four months from the word "malignant" to the quiet. After the quiet he found a table where the noise in his head finally stopped, and mistook it for peace. Every honest instinct he has left is her voice.

Nora — the daughter

Eight years old, a heart that skips a beat and forgets to catch up. She is the reason the stakes can never be waved off — and the lever the house reaches for when it wants to hurt him. Her name should appear exactly where it will cut deepest.

The spiral — the debt

Two years chasing one good night. Lies to his brother, to Nora's doctors, to himself. The debt is not the villain — it's the proof of the wound. He gambles because it's the one place grief goes quiet, and that is exactly the hook Aldric was once caught on too.

Aldric Voss was you.

A guest, once. He owed, he grieved, he was very good. He won the seven games and the house gave him everything — and then made him the prize's keeper. The winner becomes the host. The host cannot leave until someone beats him. The curse compels him to cheat, so no one ever does. He has been winning, and losing, for longer than he'll say.

So he cheats to lose.

He can't throw a game — the house won't let him. But he can be careless. He can leave a tell too long, plant a clue, sign a warning in another hand and slip it into an envelope. Every impossible thing he does is him showing his hand on purpose, praying you're sharp enough to call it. The antagonist is an accomplice. He needs you to be better than him.

THE INVITATION · one object carries the whole reveal

The letter that opens the game is also the game's Rosetta stone — but it releases its meaning in three stages, each gated by where the player is emotionally, never by a menu.

1 · ON THE ROAD

Carried in the prologue, examinable any time. Turn it over and the back is bare — only a faint impression, "as if something waits to be written." The player learns the object, and that its back is blank.

2 · INSIDE, WHEN DOUBT PEAKS

Once suspicion is high and the player wants proof, something pulls them back to the letter — a weight in the pocket, a prompt. Now handwriting has surfaced on that bare back: "He cannot lose… watch his hands. — a friend who got out." The clue that arms the Read test, delivered exactly when it's earned.

3 · THE TWIST, AT THE END

In the endgame the signature resolves one last time — "a friend who never left." The hand is Aldric's own. He wrote the warning. He is the friend. He never got out. He has been trying to free himself through you since the first envelope slid under your door.

THE TURN · the middle of the story

For the first stretch you think you're the prey — outplayed by a man who cannot be beaten, bleeding sanity, losing hands you know you won. Then the doubt sharpens into a plan, you run your own test, and you catch him. And in the crack of that first caught-green you see it: the flicker isn't rage. It's relief. The whole story re-reads in that instant. You're not fighting your way out. You're being let out — by someone who can only hold the door if you're the one who pushes.

HOW IT CAN END
The Cheat · true

You catch him enough times, follow every clue, open the hidden room — and beat him honestly. He loses at last. He is freed, the curse breaks, the ledger wipes clean, and you walk out with everything: the money, Nora's operation, the door. The one ending where the credits roll. He says one word. "Finally."

Replacement

You win the seven games without ever understanding how — take the prize, take the deal. The mask is placed on your face. You get the money. You become the next Games Master. Nora is saved by a father who will never come home.

The Hollow

Sanity reaches zero. The colour drains, the cards go blank, and in the last numberless game he goes quiet, then human — and almost helps you, and can't. No door. He tried hardest, here, and it wasn't enough.

The Debt

You lose the night. The house keeps you a while longer — and the ledger remembers, so the next run he greets the guest who reached the door and turned back. A loss is never the end. It's a bookmark.

§ 19 · Pacing & the shape of a night

Seven games, one rising curve.

Horror lives or dies on rhythm. The seven games aren't a difficulty ladder — they're an emotional one. Corruption and sanity are the two dials; the trick is to never move them in a straight line. Tension, then a breath. A scare, then a clue. The manor between games is the exhale that makes the next held breath land.

I
The Parlor

The teach. Fair, warm, honest — so the whole rest of the game has something to violate. Ends on the first slip and the seed of doubt. Corruption 0→1.

II
The Gallery

A quiet, dread-soaked breath. Memory and absence, not adrenaline. This is where the lore lands and the first secret door hides. Sanity pressure begins.

III
The Court

The first hostile spike. He turns on you — prosecutor, judge, jury, all him. This is where the player, now armed with the Read verb, first fights back and wins loudly. Corruption 2.

IV
The Labyrinth

The midpoint plunge. First person, cold, spatial. The one game that's pure fear, not fair play — and the mirror where the identity thesis lands. Deliberate palette break. Save a real jump-scare for here.

V–VI
The two you build

Escalation and complication — his cheating gets brazen, the tells get harder, the clues get bolder. One should give the player a win that feels like mastery; one should cost them something. Corruption 3.

VII
The last game

The stage admits itself. Max corruption is max desperation, not evil — he is almost free and can barely hold the mask. Which ending you get is decided by how well you learned to read him. Corruption 4.

THE LOOP OF A SINGLE SITTING

Game → Manor → Game. Every game is a held breath; every manor stretch between is the exhale — explore, find a clue, work a puzzle, open a door, hear a fragment of who you were. The manor is where the player metabolizes what just happened and chooses to go on.

Target a game at 8–15 minutes, a manor beat at 3–6. Long enough to matter, short enough to leave the player wanting the next card turned.

FOUR RULES OF RHYTHM
  • Never two scares in a row. A fright must be followed by a quiet the player can breathe in — that's where dread grows.
  • Every loss returns something. A clue, a line, a door. Failure advances the mystery even when it doesn't advance the score.
  • Reveal on the upswing. Land lore right after a win, when the player is open — not mid-panic.
  • Corruption climbs; comfort doesn't return. Once a room has curdled, it stays a shade colder. The warmth of the Parlor never fully comes back.
THE FEELING TO PROTECT ABOVE ALL

One sentence to test every scene against: "Am I sure of what I just saw?" Not gore, not volume — doubt. The scares, the sanity distortion, the cheats, the house that remembers — all of it feeds the same delicious uncertainty. When a beat doesn't deepen that doubt, cut it. When it does, you've found the game.
