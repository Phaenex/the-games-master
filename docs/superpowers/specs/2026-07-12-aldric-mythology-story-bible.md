# Aldric Voss — Mythology & Story Bible

## Status

Draft 4. Final for this design pass — reviewed on four lenses (tone/genre-fit, consistency against shipped code and Art Direction, game-design coherence, plot-hole hunting) across three total drafting passes. Revision history moved to the end of this document so the mythology itself reads as settled worldbuilding rather than a running argument with its own reviewers. This document is the foundational lore that the per-game cheat designs and the clue/secret-room design must stay consistent with.

Everything cited from `The Games Master - Art Direction.dc.html` (§1, §3, §5, §10, §11, §13, §17, §18, §19) is primary canon. This document exists to fill the gaps Art Direction left open, not to compete with what it already decided.

## 1. The Bargain

Aldric Voss was a guest once. He won. The house made him the host.

He is not trying to win. He is trying to lose — and he can't. Winning transfers the role: an ordinary win, by skill or luck or anything short of exposing the game as rigged, doesn't free a guest — it binds them to the table the way it bound him. This is already the Replacement ending, happening one loop earlier, to him.

Why this protagonist, why tonight — not fate, not a targeted choice. The house's invitation is indiscriminate; it finds the desperate because desperation is what reliably produces someone willing to say yes to an offer this strange. That the protagonist is broke and playing for someone they love, the same as Aldric once was, isn't a hidden design. It's what every guest who ever said yes has in common.

## 2. The Rule

The house's rule, not a devil's, not an enforcer's — a quasi-sentient presence, the same one already written elsewhere as "a house that said yes to everything." Aldric knows its shape: win ordinarily, inherit the role; get caught cheating past a full reckoning, and the game voids, releasing everyone at the table. He doesn't know its source, and neither does this document. A horror premise doesn't need a fully-explained cosmology, and this one is better left alone.

The previous host — the one before Aldric — is not named, not seen, not given a portrait. He exists only as a residue worn into the walls, a presence the house itself has mostly forgotten. He isn't Percival, whose portrait hangs on the wall with the other eight guest-losers and carries no more mystery than a worn nameplate and an unexplained repeat visitor. A portrait is fixed and named; the previous host is neither, and should stay that way.

## 3. Why He Can't Just Let You Win

He can't consciously choose to lose any more than he could consciously choose an ordinary win — both feed the house identically. The same rule reaches his recruitment pitch: steering a guest away from an ordinary win by hedging the invitation would itself be a form of intentionally losing, which the rule forbids as strictly as an outright collapse. That's why his opening pitch is wholehearted and appealing rather than laced with warning — *"You may have every note of it. You need only win it from me... And I will play perfectly fair."* He isn't lying. He's incapable of doing anything else. What leaks through isn't in the content of the pitch — it's in the involuntary edges around it, which is where his tells actually live (§4).

The only outcome that breaks the cycle for both parties is getting caught, decisively, past the threshold he fell short of himself, centuries ago.

## 4. What's Actually Escalating

Corruption climbs across a single playthrough (Tiers 0 through 4). Read narratively, this isn't a fresh Aldric hardening in real time — a session never actually starts at Tier 0; it defaults to Tier 1, so every guest meets him already past the baseline, on a curve that's been climbing for a long time before this particular night. Most of his history was closer to Tier 0: rare cheating, hard to catch, centuries of near-total failure to be caught at all — which is why so few names made it onto the ledger, and why the ledger was never a full census to begin with. The house's memory is already established as selective (the previous host is a forgotten residue, not a record); most guests over the centuries left no durable trace. A portrait and a ledger line is something the house grants rarely, not a guarantee.

What actually climbs with the tier, matching the game's own cheat logic: **frequency** (he plays looser and more recklessly the more unraveled he gets, so more of his hands end up needing rescue) and **visibility** (the tell gets sloppier and more legible — the IRRITATED state, gold flickering green for a frame, "relief he can't afford to show"). The trigger itself never stops being reactive — he cheats only when he's actually about to lose, at every tier. There's no point in this arc where he cheats from safety.

The tells aren't a separate, protected channel of leftover humanity holding steady while the cheating hardens around it — they're leakage from the exact same failing mechanism. One mask, failing on every seam at once. The already-shipped line *"…forgive me. Old habit, that last word. Pick up your hand. We begin."* is that failure, caught mid-sentence.

## 5. The Six Endings

No ending's conditions change here — only what they mean, read against the bargain above.

**Escape** (Defiance ≥15, Sanity ≥40) — the player leaves without triggering either trap. A survivor's ending: they got out, the cycle continues for the next guest. Aldric watches from the window, per the existing text — legible now as him watching someone else get the clean escape he never had.

**Replacement** (Defiance ≥18, an Inversion used) — *"He removes the mask — nothing behind it — and you sit in his chair."* This is the ending a player reaches by force: Defiance and dominance, no exposure, no understanding. The mask comes off because the transfer is already happening, and the transfer is what hollows it — the player hasn't earned Aldric's interior life by beating him this way, only the chair. What they see is a preview of their own future in it, not a claim that Aldric was always hollow. The bargain doesn't erase who you were the instant you win it; it erases you slowly, the way it's been erasing him this whole document.

**Pact** (balanced Defiance/Compliance) — a negotiated third option, neither escape nor damnation. What Aldric himself might have taken, once, if he'd known it existed.

**Collection** (Compliance ≥15) — the player's portrait joins the wall. One more name for a future guest's ledger to list.

**Cheat / true ending** (8+ cheats caught, 3+ shards, hidden room found) — the player succeeds exactly where Aldric fell short, centuries ago, by a margin and a hidden room he never found himself. The shipped text is simple and doesn't need help: **"Finally."** Freed, the curse breaks, credits roll. Art Direction's own thesis already does the necessary work — the two "victories" a player instinctively chases, beating him hard (Replacement) or playing nice (Collection), are the two that trap you. Only understanding gets everyone out. That's the twist. It doesn't need a second reading bolted onto the one ending that's actually clean.

**Hollow** (Sanity hits 0) — the shipped text is explicit that this is the one ending where he tries hardest to help: in the last numberless game, blank cards, no credits, he goes quiet, then human, and almost helps, and can't. He tried hardest here, and it wasn't enough. Read against the bargain, this is the starkest version of §3's rule: even a guest too far gone to keep playing doesn't get a deliberate mercy, because deliberate mercy is exactly the one move the rule has never once allowed him. No portrait, no ledger line — the house collects outcomes, and there's no one left in the room to produce one. This is the ending that leaves no trace anywhere in the house, worse than the other five specifically because it's forgotten completely, without even a worn nameplate to show for it.

## 6. The Ledger, the Nameplate, and What They Mean

The Entry Hall ledger's illegible ninth entry — worn past reading, the ink gone soft from repeated touching — sits directly above the blank line left for the player, sized to their own width. It parallels Percival's own worn nameplate, the one portrait someone kept returning to. Both are the same clue, restated once in two places rather than explained: someone came back to this name, over and over, for reasons the game never has to spell out. That's the payoff. It doesn't need a second reveal identifying whose hand wore the ink thin.

## 7. Two Different "Friends"

Art Direction uses "a friend" in two places for two different things, and they should never be allowed to blur together:

- **The invitation letter's "friend"** (§18) is Aldric, writing to himself — or to the next guest, the way he wishes someone had written to him. A concrete object: a specific hand, a three-stage reveal, a clean payoff. Fully resolved.
- **"The friend in the walls"** (§11, §2 above) is the previous host — ambient, unseen, cross-run-seeded, never confirmed, never given a face. Fully unresolved, on purpose.

Going forward, if a scene needs to reference both, the letter's signer is "a friend" (its own established term) and the ambient presence is "the friend in the walls" or "something in the walls" — never just "the friend" alone once both are live in the same sequence.

## 8. "The Mirror" Is Not the Labyrinth's Mirrors

A second naming collision worth being just as careful about: **"the Mirror"** is Art Direction's name for the secret eighth game, the NG+ mode unlocked by a completed run — a menu-level system, not a physical object in any room. The Labyrinth separately contains actual glass mirrors as its own Identity Hook, showing nothing, or the GM, or a figure a beat late. These are two unrelated things that happen to share a word. Any future clue or shard tied to "a mirror" needs to say which one — the secret game mode, or the physical objects in the Labyrinth — the same discipline as §7 above.

## 9. The Hidden Room, In Outline

Aldric's own original chamber, sealed since before he became host, forgotten by the house the way old wounds get walled over. What it contains and how it's found is specified in the clue/secret-room design, not here — this document only needs to establish that it exists, that it's his, and that finding it is a reward for having already understood most of what this document describes, not a random unlock.

## 10. Twists

Only one of these is a twist a player experiences; the rest are continuity notes worth stating plainly rather than dressed up as surprises.

1. **Aldric was once a guest who won, and winning is what trapped him.** *(§1 — Art Direction's own thesis, the one twist that actually lands. The premise itself is stock — Inscryption/Leshy-shaped — and this document doesn't argue otherwise. The mechanism is what's specific to this game.)*
2. The ledger's worn ninth entry and Percival's worn nameplate are the same clue, told twice. *(§6, a detail for attentive players, not a reveal.)*
3. Aldric's small dialogue slips are real cracks in the compulsion, not writing flourish. *(§4.)*
4. The letter's "friend" and "the friend in the walls" are two different things sharing a word, kept distinct on purpose. *(§7 — a house style rule as much as a twist.)*

## 11. Open Questions

- **Who Aldric loved or played for, originally** — deliberately unnamed. A forced parallel to the protagonist's own losses would need to be earned, not asserted.
- **The hidden room's exact contents and gate** — specified in the clue/secret-room design document, not duplicated here.
- **Ending mechanics as shipped code** — the six-ending table (§5 above, and the original design brief) is design intent. The Parlor prototype currently implements Corruption, Sanity, and a cheat counter — the substrate the endings will read off of — but none of the six endings exist as coded outcomes yet. Treat this document as the target, not something already built.

## Revision History

Kept separate from the mythology above so the lore itself doesn't read as an argument with its own review process.

- **Draft 1** invented a mythology from scratch without first checking `The Games Master - Art Direction.dc.html`, which already contained a more complete, better-integrated version of nearly the same ideas.
- **Draft 2** consolidated with Art Direction as primary canon: retired an invented "Percival is the previous host" theory (contradicts Art Direction's deliberately non-corporeal "friend in the walls"), fixed a factual error about which ledger entry is illegible (last, not first), dropped a fabricated "two readings at once" claim about the Cheat ending, and resolved a genuine internal collision in Art Direction itself between two different uses of "a friend."
- **Draft 3** incorporated a full plot-hole review: corrected the escalation model to match actual code (the cheat trigger is reactive-only at every tier, never threat-free — only frequency and visibility climb, not whether a threat is required), reconciled "nothing behind it" (Replacement) with a continuous, sympathetic Aldric, extended the "can't consciously help you" rule to cover his recruitment pitch, and added the invitation-mechanism note now in §1.
- **Draft 4** (this version) fixed a fabricated quote — an invented "the mask dissolves, resolves into the player's own face" image that doesn't exist in any shipped text — and corrected the Hollow ending's reading, which had it backwards: the shipped text says Aldric tries hardest to help in that specific ending, not that the house is indifferent. Also caught and fixed a second naming collision ("the Mirror," the secret game, versus the Labyrinth's physical mirrors — now §8), dropped an unverified claim about corruption tiers changing cheat *margin* (only frequency and visibility are actually in the code), corrected a paraphrased ending quote to the exact shipped line, and rewrote the whole document to remove changelog-style section headers and repeated re-explaining of the same point, which a tone review correctly flagged as reading like a review response rather than lore.
